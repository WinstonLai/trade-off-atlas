Business communication

Early vs. accurate updates

Raise a risk early and people have time to act, but you might be wrong. Wait until you're sure and you'll be right, but too late to help. Saying how confident you are lets you speak early without staking your credibility.

Early warningvs.Certainty

The trade-off

When you spot a problem (a slipping date, a budget overrun, a customer about to leave) you face a timing choice. Every day you wait, you learn more and your warning gets more accurate. Every day you wait, the people who could do something about it have fewer options. A warning in week one can still change the plan. The same warning in week nine is just an announcement.

What makes people wait is the cost of being wrong. A false alarm stated as fact damages credibility, so the cautious move is to stay quiet until certain. But that cost depends heavily on framing. "We will miss the date" is a claim you can be wrong about. "I think there's a 60% chance we slip; I'll know by Friday" is a report you can't be wrong about, only update. Framing a warning with an honest confidence level lowers the cost of speaking early, so the best time to speak moves earlier and the update is more useful when it arrives. This is the reasoning behind the military briefing habit of stating confidence levels, and behind "no surprises" as a rule for managing stakeholders.

How to use it

  1. Separate what you know from what you suspect. "Here's what we've seen; here's what I think it means."
  2. Give a confidence level and a date for the next update. It tells people how much weight to put on it and when they'll know more.
  3. Ask what decisions are coming up. If something irreversible is about to happen, early and uncertain beats late and certain.
  4. Follow up, especially if it turns out fine. Closing the loop on false alarms is what keeps the next warning credible.

Example

A project lead notices a supplier's test results trending badly six weeks before launch. Instead of waiting for the final report, they tell the sponsor: "About a 50% chance the supplier part fails certification. I'll know in two weeks. If it fails, our backup adds three weeks." The sponsor approves a small order of backup parts as insurance. The part does fail, and launch slips one week instead of four.

Watch out for

  • The curves here are a toy model. Real certainty doesn't grow smoothly, and some risks resolve in a single test result.
  • Crying wolf. Many early, low-confidence warnings that never come true still wear down attention. Share early what is decision-relevant, not every worry.
  • Organisations that punish bad news. If messengers get blamed, people will wait. Leaders who want early warnings have to reward them, including the false ones.
