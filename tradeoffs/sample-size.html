Sample size: precision vs. cost

More data makes an estimate more precise, but with steeply falling returns: halving the margin of error takes four times the sample. The right size depends on the smallest difference that would change your decision.

Precisionvs.Cost

The trade-off

The random error in an estimate shrinks with the square root of the sample size. For a survey result near 50%, the 95% margin of error is roughly ±98/√n percentage points: about ±14 with 50 responses, ±5 with 385, ±2.5 with about 1,500 and ±1 with nearly 10,000. Cost, on the other hand, usually grows in a straight line with n. So the first few hundred responses buy a lot of precision, and every halving of the margin after that costs four times as much.

That makes "how big a sample?" a business question as much as a statistical one. The useful starting point is the smallest difference that would change what you do. If you'll act only on a 10-point gap, a few hundred responses is enough and thousands is waste. If a 1-point change in conversion is worth millions, a huge sample is cheap by comparison. Power analysis formalises this for experiments: pick the effect size worth detecting, then compute the n that detects it reliably.

How to use it

  1. Start from the decision. What difference matters? What happens if you're wrong?
  2. Work out the required n before collecting data. Collecting until the result looks good is a different, and biased, procedure.
  3. Spend on quality once n is enough. Past a few thousand, improving who responds usually helps more than more responses.
  4. Size for subgroups if you'll report them. A good margin overall can become a poor one for each region or segment.

Example

A team planned a 10,000-person survey to choose between two pricing pages. Asked what result would change their decision, they agreed that only a gap of 10 points or more would matter. About 400 responses per page was enough to see that, which cut the cost by over 90%. They spent part of the savings on recruiting a sample that actually matched their customer base.

Watch out for

  • Margin of error covers only random error. It says nothing about bias. In 1936 the Literary Digest poll collected over two million responses and still called the US presidential election badly wrong, because of who it asked.
  • The formula assumes a simple random sample. Clustered or weighted samples have larger real margins.
  • Huge samples make tiny effects "significant". With enough data, a meaningless difference passes a significance test. Look at the size of the effect too.
  • Related: error rates in experiments are significance vs. power, how to divide the data you have is training vs. test data, and checking results as data comes in is peeking vs. valid results.
