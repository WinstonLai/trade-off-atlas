The trade-off

A significance test at the 5% level is designed so that, when nothing is really going on, it says "significant" one time in twenty. That's fine for one pre-chosen test. But experiments rarely track one metric: there are conversion, revenue, retention, clicks, each split by device and country. Test 20 independent metrics with no real effect and the chance that at least one looks significant is 1 − 0.9520, about 64%. Somebody will find a "win".

Corrections fix this by raising the bar. The Bonferroni correction tests each of m metrics at 5% ÷ m, which keeps the chance of any false alarm under 5%, but makes real effects much harder to detect. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (1995) controls something different: the expected share of discoveries that are false (the false discovery rate). It allows a few more false alarms in exchange for finding more real effects, which is why it's standard in genomics and widely used for experiment dashboards. Either way, looking at more things costs you, in false alarms or in power.

How to use it

  1. Pick one primary metric in advance. It gets the full 5% test. Everything else is supporting evidence.
  2. Correct when you scan many metrics. Bonferroni when any false claim is costly, Benjamini–Hochberg when you're screening for leads.
  3. Count every comparison you made. Segments, time windows and metrics you checked but didn't report all count.
  4. Treat surprising secondary wins as hypotheses. Confirm them with a new experiment before acting.

Example

A new onboarding flow showed no change in sign-ups, but the dashboard highlighted a significant rise in sign-ups from tablets in Germany. Across the 40 device-and-country segments being checked, one or two such results were expected by chance. The team reran the test with German tablet users as the pre-registered segment, and the effect disappeared.

Watch out for

  • The model here simplifies. It assumes independent metrics and a fixed share of real effects. Correlated metrics inflate false alarms less than the formula suggests.
  • The garden of forking paths. Andrew Gelman's term for the many analysis choices (which outliers to drop, which period to use) that act like extra comparisons even when only one test is run.
  • Over-correcting. Bonferroni across hundreds of metrics can make it nearly impossible to detect anything. Decide in advance which questions matter.
  • Related: false alarms vs. misses for one test is significance vs. power, the timing version of this problem is peeking vs. valid results, and gaming a metric is proxy metrics vs. the real goal.
