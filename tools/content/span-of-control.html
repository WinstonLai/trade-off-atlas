The trade-off

Span of control is the number of people who report directly to one manager. It sets the shape of the organisation: for a fixed headcount, narrow spans mean many layers and wide spans mean few. Every layer adds a hand-off. Decisions and escalations climb further, information is filtered more times, and there are more managers to pay.

But a manager's time is finite. Spread across four people it allows close coaching. Spread across fifteen it allows little more than a weekly check-in. So flatter is faster but thinner. Classic management writers put the limit at around five to seven reports. Later work, including Gallup's research on managers, found that wider spans can work well when people are experienced, the work is routine, and decisions are pushed down to the team, and that they fail when people need frequent guidance.

How to use it

  1. Start from what people need. New or junior staff, complex work and frequent change all call for narrower spans.
  2. Count the layers a decision climbs. If routine approvals need three levels, spans are probably too narrow.
  3. Widen spans by changing the work, not just the chart. Clear goals, delegated decisions and good tools let a manager support more people.
  4. Watch the symptoms. Slow decisions and many "managers of one" point to spans that are too narrow; burnt-out managers and unsupported staff point to spans that are too wide.

Example

A 200-person engineering group with four reports per manager had four layers of management, and a design question could take a week to reach someone who could decide. A reorganisation moved to spans of about ten, removing a layer. It worked for the experienced teams. A team of recent graduates struggled, so it was split again with a dedicated lead for every five people.

Watch out for

  • The model here is simplified. Hours per report and layers are a rough proxy. Managers also do project work, hiring and planning, which changes the real numbers.
  • Flattening without delegating. Removing a layer while still routing every decision to the top just creates a bottleneck at a higher level.
  • One size for every team. Spans can differ across the same organisation, and probably should.
