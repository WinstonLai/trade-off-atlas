The trade-off

People act on confident advice. In the short run, the advisor who says "definitely" beats the one who says "probably", which researchers call the confidence heuristic. So there is a pull to sound more certain than you are. The opposite habit is just as common: wrapping every statement in "might", "possibly" and "it depends" so that you can never be caught out. That is safe, but nobody can act on it.

The balance shifts once people can check your record. Elizabeth Tenney and colleagues found that when people learned how accurate advisors had actually been, they trusted well-calibrated advisors (whose confidence matched their accuracy) over confident but often-wrong ones. Philip Tetlock's forecasting research points the same way: the best forecasters are the calibrated ones, not the boldest. So overclaiming is a bet that nobody keeps score. Where they do, influence peaks when stated confidence matches how often you're right.

How to use it

  1. Use numbers or clear words for confidence. "About 80%" or "very likely" carries more information than "I think".
  2. Be confident where you have earned it. Hedging things you know well wastes your credibility as much as overclaiming does.
  3. Keep your own score. Write down predictions and check them. It's the only way to know whether you are calibrated.
  4. Hedge once, clearly. One explicit caveat beats a qualifier in every sentence.

Example

Two analysts forecast quarterly demand. One always speaks with total certainty, the other gives ranges and confidence levels. For a year the confident one gets more airtime. Then the head of planning compares their forecasts with what happened: the confident analyst missed by a wide margin a third of the time, while the other's "80% likely" calls came true about 80% of the time. From then on, the planning team asks the second analyst first.

Watch out for

  • The curve here is a model. The trust penalty and its size are illustrative. Real reactions depend on how costly the misses were.
  • Overconfidence pays in some places. In settings without feedback (one-off pitches, distant forecasts) bluster can win. That is a reason to build feedback in, not to bluster.
  • Different listeners read hedges differently. "Possibly" can mean 10% to one person and 60% to another. Numbers avoid that.
