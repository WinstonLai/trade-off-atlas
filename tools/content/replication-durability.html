The trade-off

Disks fail: a few percent of them each year, according to large published fleet studies such as Backblaze's. With one copy of the data, that is the chance of losing it. With two copies on different disks, data is lost only if the second disk fails during the hours it takes to rebuild the first. Because that window is short, each extra copy multiplies durability enormously. Three copies is the long-standing default in systems like HDFS and many object stores.

But each copy costs a full copy's worth of storage, and the maths assumes failures are independent. They often aren't: disks in the same rack share power and network, disks from the same batch share firmware bugs, and a data centre can flood. When copies share a fate, a third copy in the same place buys far less than a third copy somewhere else. Erasure coding offers another point on the curve: splitting data into fragments plus parity (for example 6 data + 3 parity) survives several failures for about 1.5× the storage instead of 3×, at the cost of more CPU and network to rebuild.

How to use it

  1. Spread copies across failure domains. Different racks first, different zones or regions for data you can't lose.
  2. Shorten the repair window. Faster detection and rebuilds shrink the time in which a second failure is fatal.
  3. Match the scheme to the data. Replicate hot data for fast reads; erasure-code cold data to save storage.
  4. Remember that replicas aren't backups. A deleted or corrupted file is deleted on every copy. Keep separate, versioned backups.

Example

A team ran a three-way replicated database cluster and assumed it was safe. During a power fault, one rack went down, and it turned out two of the three replicas for several shards lived in that rack. Data survived on the remaining copy, but only just. They reconfigured placement so that no two replicas share a rack, and moved archives to erasure-coded storage, which halved their storage cost for that data.

Watch out for

  • The probabilities here come from a simplified model. Real systems have correlated failures, silent corruption and human error, which this formula ignores.
  • "Nines" can mislead. Eleven nines of durability per object still means some loss across billions of objects, and says nothing about availability.
  • Copies cost more than disk space. Every write goes to every replica, which costs network bandwidth and can slow writes down.
  • Related: what replicas agree on is consistency vs. latency, behaviour when the network splits is the CAP theorem, and how data is divided across nodes is hash vs. range partitioning.
