The trade-off

A message can lean on evidence (figures, charts, method) or on narrative (a specific customer, a concrete scene, a before and after). Evidence is hard to argue with but hard to remember: an audience that nodded through twelve charts can rarely repeat one of them an hour later. Stories stick, which is the central argument of Chip and Dan Heath's Made to Stick, but a single example is easy to wave away as an exception.

Combining them usually beats either alone: a story to make people care and remember, and a number to show the story is typical. But the mix depends on the audience. Analysts and specialists discount anecdotes and want to see the method. Executives want the one number and an example they can retell. A general audience responds to people more than percentages. Research by Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic found that people gave more to help one named child than in response to statistics about millions, and that adding the statistics to the child's story actually reduced giving. So more data is not always more persuasive.

How to use it

  1. Lead with one concrete case. A specific customer, transaction or incident gives the numbers something to attach to.
  2. Back it with the number that shows it's typical. "Sara's order took nine days. So did 30% of orders last month."
  3. Adjust for the room. Add method and caveats for experts; cut to one figure for a broad audience.
  4. Put the rest in an appendix. Detail that supports the case doesn't need to be in the main message.

Example

A product manager's first pitch for a checkout redesign was fifteen slides of funnel metrics. Leadership agreed it was interesting and moved on. The second pitch opened with a thirty-second screen recording of a real customer failing to apply a discount code three times, then showed one number: 18% of carts with a code are abandoned at that step. The project was funded that afternoon.

Watch out for

  • The curves here are illustrative. The audience weights are a simplification; real rooms are mixed.
  • Cherry-picked stories. A vivid example that isn't typical is misleading, however well told. Check that your story matches your data.
  • Stories can carry weak cases. The power that makes narrative persuasive also makes it dangerous. When you're the audience, ask for the number behind the anecdote.
